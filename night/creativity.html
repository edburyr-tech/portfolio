Teaching Creativity

 


There is a saying that you can’t teach creativity. I am going to attempt to disprove this with the aid of games design teaching examples.

Originality through Team Work

There is a simple game children play where they tell a story by taking it in turns to say one word. I have used this in teaching and while the stories are often obscene, they are always original. These ideas are often a bit bizarre and it’s hard to imagine making good games out of most of them, but it is clear that even a simple interaction can spark creativity in people who might otherwise claim to be uncreative.

Pitch part of an idea (or better yet get a student to).  Get others to develop that idea. This can be iterated, so the ideas people come up with can then be developed further. When doing this with four groups I’ve always returned at least three very distinctly different ideas.

The idea below was generated this way and came about when someone I was working with stated absolutely “all psychopaths wear sandals”.  It’s key to point out that in many situations this conversation would have run aground with this person either being ignored or shut down. I would suggest two rules for all these activities, everyone participates and no shutting down or mocking ideas.

The conversation swiftly developed into a discussion of a killer who has these bizarre signature things they do at every crime scene such as placing the victims head into a goldfish bowl or indeed wearing flip flops. Soon scoring for a variety of these traits emerged and a bizarre dark humorous idea was born. 

Sim Pscyho

At first you play through the rose tinted eyes of a deranged Psychopath. In this game mode the graphics are tinted, happy and cartoony, the atrocities you commit are not. Playing like a macabre and humorous version of the Hitman franchise you hunt down you prey and kill them aiming to maximise your Psychotic Trait Points whilst avoiding capture.

The second part follows the detective pair investigating the murder. Artwork here is in full gory detail, hard hitting and realistic. With a different humour style although still very dark, the game play follows the point and click adventure mould with a few extras.

The plot culminates with the discovery that the detective you play and the psychopath are the same person just with split personalities!

Sometimes you will be left without a friend to brainstorm with; a similar idea sparking method can be done via a Google image search. Search for a word that will not give too many identical results such as “painting”, “fiction” or “landscape” and scroll through the images until one triggers an idea you can work with.

What if … Method

What if nobody could lie? What if there is a nuclear war? What if I was sent back in time? What if became a vampire?  Come up with a situation, what if … happened, extrapolate from there and you can come up with some very cool ideas. As you may have noticed the first scenario is the Ricky Gervais film “The Invention of Lying” and there are numerous other examples from games, books and film of this method in use. One of the most recent is the film The Purge which asks; what if all crime was legal for one day a year?

As a teaching activity I recommend first giving the group a “what if”, such as, what if the Nazi’s won world war 2. Follow this up by getting them to come up some, “what if” scenarios of their own.

Fun First Method

This method comes about from playing games where the developers get you to do some very boring things. When you design a game you want something that will sell and normally the best way to make a game that sells, is to make a game that is actually fun to play.

Simply, you pick something that makes a game fun and you then try to make a game that optimises this trait. Some examples fun things include manual dexterity, social interaction, altruism, winning it and player immersion. For the more advanced you might want to consider optimising two or more of these things.

Interestingly the top value is often not best, for example a game that you always win, might be less fun than a game you win 95% of the time and a game that is as quick as possible, may be too fast to control. A good classroom project might be to get students to experiment to find the optimal values and then design a game to match the value they come up with.

The Very, Very Fast Game

Very few games have done speed well over the years.  Most games focusing on speed become uncontrollable, memory exercises or force people not to use the maximum speed. Sonic whilst not perfect for not forcing you to stop has several main features to stop you slowing down,  level design, the partial immunity granted by spinning and the fact that most things cause you to lose rings rather than die (and you can normally recollect at least one ring meaning you are safe). My game applies similar principles to the car based fighting game.

So the basic principles to encourage players to move quickly:

  1. Immunity if you are going over a certain speed (but collisions slow you down).
  2. Occasional race scenario.
  3. Mini maps or other clear indications of upcoming obstacles.
  4. Nothing that actually punishes you for going quickly.

As a note I could include time limits, I feel a hard time limit is often very annoying, although softer time limits such as where a player is driving through an area being rent asunder by an earthquake could be a lot of fun.

The game I have envisaged would be an above view (as this allows the player to see more around them) driving based roleplaying game where the vehicles a player has access to upgrade and many are very quick. While some scenarios could be races I see most as having rival cars tanks etc. trying to kill you while you fulfil an objective.

I’d like to include combos in the game, not of the complicated button press kind but enemies killed with no breaks and other similar challenges to increase the manual dexterity factor. I’d also like to have a lot of interactive scenery for example two parallel bars that you would need to build up speed and turn horizontally to cross, another of Sonic’s strengths is providing players with fun scenery to be fast on such as the pinball style bumpers, jumps and loop the loops most of which can be directly transposed.

Free Writing

Normally when you write you have a plan and are trying to steer towards an end goal. If you write quickly without stopping and with no revision you will often find your story takes turns you did not expect. Putting two fairly strongly defined characters in an interesting situation and have them act as those characters would, may well result in some situations that are unexpected.

As a note this can be done with acting too, although this can be hard for many gaming students.

Path of least Resistance

The path of least resistance is to build on an existing idea, but it often turns out this is harder for students than it looks, especially as they generally pick a very polished product to evolve. At the basic level you want to identify what you like about something and what you dislike and then find a solution for the things you dislike, whilst keeping what you like.

I’ve had this solution several times:

Game: Call of Duty.

What I dislike: lag.

Solution: make it lag less.

When I teach this I generally tie it in with contextual analysis and reviewing and get students to correct a game based on a review. The other activity I like is that of merging two reasonably different products together. I like fries, I like ice-cream, mmm fries and ice-cream.

Tarot Story based game

This is an example of taking something existing and making it into a game in this case Tarot.
Fortune telling is a great story device, you can give away hints, add mystery and talk about a subject that many people find inherently interesting. Tarot cards have long been used for fortune telling but originally started out as a game. My concept was: While playing the game tarot the fortunes told by the cards come true.

I chose to have the player be a tarot player in a castle, playing against some of the rulers of the land. While other events occur and there may occasionally be cut scenes between games, most of the story and various intrigues at court are told through dialogue while playing the game. This section is based on the Hand of the king’s council meetings in the Game of Thrones books.

One slight problem is that the tarot game itself may be very poor but I think this could easily be solved and potentially you could even have a strategy card game with a tarot theme or give players the opportunity to play the tarot game of yore.

This game has the advantage for independent developers that tarot card artwork that is in the public domain exists, although personally when I had a quick look I couldn’t find artwork that satisfied me. One note though is that a designer is always looking for ways to save work for the team, as that allows more time to make the game better in other ways.

Do I need creativity?

A lot of people feel that they aren’t creative and may still say after doing all these techniques that their ideas suck etc. Fortunately it doesn’t matter as much as all that, many games are direct ports of real life popular things, sports, film, board games are frequently used as inspiration for games. As an example, Blizzard has admitted that the Warcraft franchise used Warhammer games as inspiration. Even top designers should be on the lookout for new trends and ideas with game potential. There will probably be a lot of creativity needed although you may find it easier with an overall concept in place already*.

There are a lot of problems with this approach, firstly it takes a long time to make a game and if your thing is a short term trend, the trend may have passed by the time the game releases. Many of the better ideas are taken already, some people have become jaded due to playing a lot of poorly made licensed products and avoid them and there can be costs or risk of legal action involved.

One tip from my time at EIDOS is that there can be some inventive ways to license the product you want cheaply. Lego Star Wars for example presumably makes use of the fact that Lego’s toy license allowed a work round of Star Wars licensing. Similarly does something have a book, film and comic well maybe those licenses are held by different people and you can see who offers it with the best terms. Also older products may be out of copyright although you might need to be careful as copyright laws vary from country to country.

You can also consider changing the setting for existing games or updating them for new technology. Would a Mario style game work well on Xbox? Would a gyms running machine be a good setting for a racing game?
Two products that I’m not aware of game ports as of yet that I think are good prospects:

The King killer Chronicles series by Patrick Rothfuss.

The board game Ricochet Robot (a very good phone application I suspect).

Normally a good game is an amalgamation of several ideas. Store your good ideas and see if they work together when you get a new one. Most games designers recognise that click and the excitement when an idea fits into place. Much of being creative is just thinking about potential applications, when something happens or you view a new product.

*Note a good exercise is to develop an existing idea, either a real game that all the students have played or one of their own ideas by adding in a level or enemies to it. Some students are a lot more creative if you narrow the scope a little.
